# Measure first: building a mixer that hears

The best automatic mixers and masterers, commercial and academic, are not clever listeners. They measure, compare against a target and set a short chain of ordinary processors, and the evidence says that well-built version of this beats or matches the neural state of the art on real sessions. Simple loudness and rule-based balancing tied the strongest generative mixer (MEGAMI) and scored about three times higher than the parameter-predicting Diff-MST in the one independent 2026 listening test ([Shi et al., ISMIR 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.02835v1)). Open-source reference matching (Matchering) finished third of twelve in a 472-listener blind mastering test, behind two human engineers and ahead of Ozone and LANDR ([MusicTech](https://musictech.com/news/gear/benn-jordan-ai-mastering-study/)). Your assistant sounds generic because on the normal mixing path it really is guessing: `get_mixer` shows only structure, with no levels, loudness or spectrum ([src/suites/music/mixer/tool-edits.ts](../src/suites/music/mixer/tool-edits.ts)). The research on LLM mixing agrees on the fix: the LLM is the brain, and measurement plus a render-and-compare loop are its ears ([InstructFX2FX, DAFx26](https://www.dafx.de/paper-archive/2026/papers/DAFx26_demo_74.pdf)). For YTStudio, the recommended build has four layers. A deterministic, role-aware rule engine writes ordinary, undoable Pro-Q / Pro-C / reverb / YT-L settings. A generalisation of the vocal render→measure→compare loop you already have checks every proposal. A Matchering-style mastering stage targets a reference or a loudness level. The LLM on top sets intent and translates words into measurable targets. Machine learning comes last, only where the rules measurably fall short. The evidence supports "reliably decent and editable". It does not yet support "better than a good engineer", and nobody has published a listening test of an LLM mixing a full multitrack song.

## Every commercial assistant runs the same analyse–target–chain loop

Under the marketing, almost every product has the same skeleton. It analyses a loud section, picks a target (a genre curve built from commercial masters, a reference track, or a perceptual ideal), sets a mostly fixed-order chain of EQ, dynamics, width, saturation and limiter, and hands the user a few macro controls. iZotope's only detailed public account (the Ozone 8 Master Assistant docs, about 2017) describes it plainly. A neural classifier scores the audio against ten genre target curves and **blends them by similarity (for example 60% pop, 30% rock, 10% country)**. EQ fits a low shelf, a high shelf and up to six bells toward that curve. Compression switches on by crest factor ("ideal" 3–10). The maximizer threshold is learned to hit **−14 LUFS with a −1 dB ceiling for streaming**. A dynamic EQ goes on the frequencies that hit the limiter hardest ([iZotope Ozone 8 docs](https://downloads.izotope.com/docs/ozone8/master-assistant/index.html)). Ozone 12 scales this up to 35 genres "generated from the latest chart-topping hits", adds reference targets and macros (Tonal Balance, Dynamic Match, Width Match, Clarity, Stabilizer, Vocal Balance) ([iZotope](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/how-to-use-master-assistant-in-ozone.html)), and adds ML stem EQ and a transient-restoring Unlimiter ([iZotope blog](https://www.izotope.com/community/blog/ozone-12)). iZotope's own published research points to how the "move toward the curve" step is probably done: a network predicts parametric EQ settings, trained through differentiable biquads against a magnitude-response loss, because a parameter-only loss failed ([Nercessian, DAFx 2020](https://dafx.de/paper-archive/details/lpNI_8szg2jo_eJO1RnKxw); [Mockenhaupt et al., DAFx 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.16691v1)).

The mixing side is thinner than the marketing suggests. Neutron's whole-mix assistant is essentially **a static level balance**. Plugin instances on every track report to a master instance, which listens to the whole song, sorts tracks into Focus, Voice, Bass, Percussion and Musical groups, and writes gains ([iZotope, Meet the Assistants](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/meet-the-izotope-assistants.html)). Its per-track assistant builds an EQ/compression/saturation chain from a short analysis. Sound On Sound found the track-level suggestions restrained and good, and the bus suggestions sometimes "a little too aggressive". There is no reverb or delay in the suite ([Sound On Sound](https://soundonsound.com/reviews/izotope-neutron-5)). Sonible's smart:EQ shares spectra across up to 10 instances with front/middle/back priorities, so lower-priority tracks get cut where they mask higher ones ([Sonible](https://www.sonible.com/smarteq4/)). Nectar's Unmask attenuates the vocal's key frequencies on other tracks, optionally only while the vocal plays ([iZotope](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/meet-the-izotope-assistants.html)).

LANDR and RoEx both come out of Joshua Reiss's group at Queen Mary, and their patents describe the classical architecture in the open. LANDR's 2012 patent extracts per-track loudness (modified BS.1770 with silence gates), spectral centroid, crest factor and loudness range. Its rules include faders "towards equal perceived loudness", brighter sources panned wider, EQ so source bandwidths don't overlap, compression that minimises loudness-range variation, and GCC-PHAT phase alignment ([US9654869B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9654869B2/en)). The 2014 semantic patent adds genre/instrument classification and **k-nearest-neighbour lookup in a database of real commercial productions**, which supplies the chain, parameters and targets. It also has explicit static rules such as "if kick drum is present, then put equalizer on bass guitar at 100 Hz, gain at −3 dB, Q 2.2" ([US20150066481A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US20150066481A1/en)). RoEx Automix asks you to label each stem's instrument "so the system understands how each should traditionally sit within the mix". It then sets level, pan, EQ, compression and reverb, and can export a DAW project ([MusicTech](https://musictech.com/news/gear/roex-automix-ai-mixing-instagram-filter/)). Its founders' masking-minimisation paper, an MPEG-model masking metric optimised by particle swarm with subgroups, was withdrawn over IP just before the company formed ([arXiv 1803.09960](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.09960v3)). That strongly suggests it is the product's core.

Gullfoss is the one real outlier. It does no target matching. It is an EQ re-solved about 300 times a second from a perceptual masking model, with "Recover" boosting masked components, "Tame" cutting dominant ones, and loudness preserved ([Gullfoss manual](https://www.soundtheory.com/static/Gullfoss%20Operation%20Manual.pdf)). Soundtheory says it uses no machine learning ([Attack Magazine](https://attackmagazine.com/?p=131301)). Ozone's Clarity does something similar, adapting the response toward pink noise above 300 Hz ([Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/izotope-ozone-11-advanced)).

How good are they? Reviewers converge on "good starting point". Ozone 12's assistant is "normally acceptable but rarely perfect" ([MusicTech](https://musictech.com/reviews/plug-ins/izotope-ozone-12-review/)). Logic's is "actually very effective" for streaming but "no substitute for an experienced mastering engineer" ([Sound On Sound](https://soundonsound.com/techniques/logic-pros-ai-mastering-assistant)). In Benn Jordan's 472-listener double-blind mastering test, two humans placed first and second, **Matchering 2.0 third, Ozone + Neutron fourth, Ozone 11 seventh**, and LANDR was disqualified ([MusicTech](https://musictech.com/news/gear/benn-jordan-ai-mastering-study/)). Two lessons transfer directly. First, plain reference matching (loudness plus a smoothed spectrum) competes with proprietary "AI" when the reference is good. Second, every serious product ships **gain-matched A/B**, because louder always sounds better and fools every listener, human or machine.

## Simple rules still match or beat neural mixers on real sessions

### The rule base is concrete, cheap and validated better than its age suggests

Queen Mary's knowledge-engineered work (2007–2018) gives a citable rule base, and these are the numbers to start from. Real engineers' mixes put the **lead vocal at −2.7 ± 1.6 LU relative to the mix**, which contradicts the "equal loudness for everything" assumption many auto-faders use ([De Man et al., ISMIR 2014](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2014/paper/000293.pdf)). The revised rules: vocal around −3 LU, total drums −6.5 to −8.5 LU, bass −8 to −9 LU, and total reverb about **−14 LU, erring toward too little**. Mixes with reverb at about −9 LU or above were judged too wet ([De Man PhD thesis](http://www.brechtdeman.com/publications/pdf/PhD-thesis.pdf)). The processor order runs HPF (so rumble doesn't drive the compressor), then compressor, EQ, fader and pan, with a −3 dB sine/cosine pan law. Low sources, kick, snare, bass and lead stay centred, and sources pan wider as frequency rises between 50 and 400 Hz (same thesis). Compression has closed-form settings from features, from Ma et al. 2015:

- ratio = 0.54·percussivity + 0.764·low-frequency weighting + 1;
- threshold = −11.03 + 0.44·RMS − 4.987·percussivity;
- attack and release scale with 1/crest²;
- make-up gain preserves loudness.

That system beat semi-professional mixes, and the equal-loudness alternative came last ([Ma et al. 2015](https://joshreiss.github.io/documents/2015/Ma%202015%20-%20Intelligent%20Multitrack%20Compression.pdf)). Light compression aimed at loudness range tied with an expert, while heavy compression was rated poor ([Maddams et al., DAFx-12](https://www.dafx.de/paper-archive/2012/papers/dafx12_submission_6.pdf)). Loudness should be measured only while a track is active, with a hysteresis gate opening at −25 and closing at −30 LUFS ([Mansbridge et al. 2012](https://joshreiss.github.io/documents/2012/MansbridgeFinnReiss-AES133-Autonomoussystemformultitrackstereopositioning.pdf)).

The headline result is that **KEAMS, a rule engine that knows each track's instrument, showed no significant preference difference from professional mixes**, and it beat an instrument-agnostic automatic mixer ([De Man thesis](http://www.brechtdeman.com/publications/pdf/PhD-thesis.pdf)). Its failure mode is just as instructive. It applied literature EQ blindly ("boost the guitar at X" on guitars that were already bright), so EQ rules must be candidates gated by measurement, never applied by rote. Masking-driven EQ came out near professional and better than amateur, but its authors found that objective masking measures predicted preference poorly ([Hafezi & Reiss 2015](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/277935370_Autonomous_Multitrack_Equalization_Based_on_Masking_Reduction)). Unmasking should therefore be gentle. Peer reviews of mixes show where the effort should go: **35% of criticism concerns level/balance and 29% space (mostly reverb)**, against 25% spectral and 11% dynamics ([De Man thesis](http://www.brechtdeman.com/publications/pdf/PhD-thesis.pdf)). Balance and reverb amount matter more than clever EQ.

Two caveats. These listening tests were small: KEAMS's discussion covered 15 subjects on a handful of songs. And the targets come from rock and pop multitracks. Nothing in the evidence says a trap beat wants its 808 at −8 LU, so the targets must be adjustable per genre and, better, learned from references the user picks.

### The deep-learning frontier is impressive, research-licensed and not yet ahead

The neural field splits into three families, and none of them is ready to be your engine.

**Direct audio-to-audio models** (Mix-Wave-U-Net, Sony's FxNorm-Automix, Koo's MixFXcloner) output mixed audio with no parameters. FxNorm and MixFXcloner are MIT with weights ([FxNorm-automix](https://github.com/sony/FxNorm-automix); [music_mixing_style_transfer](https://github.com/jhtonyKoo/music_mixing_style_transfer)), but listeners penalised their artifacts, and their output can't be edited in your Pro-Q ([MEGAMI, arXiv 2511.08040](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.08040)).

**Parameter estimators** have the right output shape. Diff-MST predicts per-track gain, EQ, compressor and pan plus a master EQ and compressor, from a reference song, for any number of tracks ([arXiv 2407.08889](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08889v1)). But it ran no listening test of its own, has no reverb, and is CC BY-NC-SA. In the independent 2026 test on dense 23–27-track sessions it scored **about 20/100 against about 55–61** for MEGAMI and a training-free equal-local-loudness balancer ([Shi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.02835v1)). ST-ITO avoids training entirely. It searches the parameters of any chain, including non-differentiable effects, with a gradient-free optimiser against a learned production-style embedding, AFx-Rep ([arXiv 2410.21233](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21233v1)). The catch is that pure embedding-distance search drifts to unrealistic settings unless it is held by a prior on plausible presets ([arXiv 2505.11315](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.11315v1)). Your own pilot reproduced exactly this. ST-ITO over native renders cut held-out distance by about 62% versus the best random start **on delay**, but cross-song EQ and chain search chose a +2.26 dB shelf where the reference used −2 dB, and lost to the best random start on held-out audio ([docs/audio-intelligence/st-ito-pilot.md](../docs/audio-intelligence/st-ito-pilot.md)).

**Generative models** lead the field. MEGAMI (Sony AI/Aalto, Nov 2025) uses diffusion to generate a per-track effect embedding, conditioned on CLAP embeddings of the dry tracks, and a neural processor renders it. It trains on about 20k songs of internal wet stems, and in a 12-listener test it "approaches human-mix quality". But confidence intervals were wide, results were song-dependent, equal loudness did "unexpectedly well" on one song, and the output is audio, not parameters ([arXiv 2511.08040](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.08040)). Its public weights are CC BY-NC-SA ([SonyResearch/MEGAMI](https://github.com/SonyResearch/MEGAMI)). Diff2Mix (ISMIR 2026) pairs diffusion with an explicit console, which is architecturally closest to what you want, but it gives no numbers and its weight status is unknown ([arXiv 2608.05442](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.05442)).

The most useful new finding is structural rather than a model. **Mixing in two stages, balancing within functional groups first and then across groups, significantly improved both MEGAMI (54.65 → 61.57) and Diff-MST**. Bad grouping hurt more than loudness errors: MEGAMI scored 65.39 with 7 groups and 50.59 with 4 ([Shi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.02835v1)).

| Approach | Output | Best evidence vs listeners | Licence | Role for YTStudio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Role-aware rules (KEAMS, Ma 2015) | parameters | ≈ pro mixes, small test ([thesis](http://www.brechtdeman.com/publications/pdf/PhD-thesis.pdf)) | papers = spec; reimplement | **The engine** |
| Equal local loudness + grouping | gains | 61 vs MEGAMI 55, n.s. ([Shi 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.02835v1)) | trivial | **The floor every change must beat** |
| ST-ITO search | parameters, any chain | strong on delay in your pilot | code licence unverified | Bounded search for delay/reverb only |
| Diff-MST | parameters | ≈20/100 independently | CC BY-NC-SA | Skip |
| MEGAMI | audio | "approaches human" (12 listeners) | CC BY-NC-SA | Dev-only comparison target, never shipped |
| FxNorm / MixFXcloner | audio | artifacts penalised | MIT (MUSDB-trained) | Baseline at most |

Data is the binding constraint. MedleyDB and MoisesDB are non-commercial and MUSDB18 is research-only ([Moises](https://music.ai/blog/news/introducing-moisesdb-the-ultimate-multitrack-dataset-for-source-separation-beyond-4-stems); [MedleyDB](https://steinhardt.nyu.edu/marl/research/resources/medleydb)). The scalable workaround in 2026 papers is synthetic: render random effect settings through your own processors to get unlimited (dry, wet, parameters) triples, the StemFX/MultiAFx recipe ([arXiv 2607.15634](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15634)). YTStudio's 45× real-time offline engine is unusually well placed to do this ([docs/fl-studio/export.md](../docs/fl-studio/export.md)), but only once rules have plateaued.

## An LLM needs meters and a critic, not ears

### What the LLM-mixing papers actually show

The LLM line moved fast, and it keeps learning the same lesson. **LLM2Fx** showed that zero-shot LLMs map timbre words to EQ well, with GPT-4o at 0.22 embedding distance against 0.55 for CLAP optimisation, but they are poor at reverb (0.70). Giving the model DSP features, the DSP code and a few examples in context gave the best results on both ([arXiv 2505.20770](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20770v2)). **LLM2Fx-Tools** fine-tuned Qwen3-4B to "listen" through Fx-Encoder++, reason, then emit tool calls. It reached a MUSHRA score of 62.8, against 56.5 for Gemini 2.5 Flash and 81.5 for the hidden reference, and removing chain-of-thought dropped chain accuracy from 80% to 67%. Like every paper in this line, it works on single instruments, not full mixes ([arXiv 2512.01559](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.01559v1)). **InstructFX2FX** states the design rule outright. The LLM "selects effects and proposes the initial parameter state", and a CLAP-guided optimiser refines it, because naive reprompting "regenerates parameters non-deterministically, may perturb settings the user did not intend to change… and cannot hear the sound it is editing". The optimiser won on 9 of 10 descriptor pairs ([DAFx26](https://www.dafx.de/paper-archive/2026/papers/DAFx26_demo_74.pdf)). **RIME** exposes 20+ studio operators over MCP, the same architecture as yours. Parameter recovery fell from **74.6% on explicit instructions to 10.9% on abstract ones** like "make it punchier" ([arXiv 2607.19605](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.19605)). So the LLM's job is to turn "punchier" into something measurable, not to guess the numbers itself. In MixAssist, producers said the agent's weakest part was its audio analysis ("It would have been more helpful had it been able to analyze the audio"), and they wanted the "why", shown inside the DAW ([arXiv 2507.06329](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06329)).

**MixJudge** (Oct 2026) is the most directly reusable result. It is a small CLAP-based critic that takes the backing, the vocal and their blend, and flags 11 issues: too loud or quiet, muddy or thin, harsh or dull, too wet or dry, over- or under-compressed, and masking. Used to choose among **31 rendered processing candidates** for a raw vocal, it picked an improving one 77.1% of the time and recovered about two-thirds of the gap to a professional vocal mix. The best general audio-language model recovered about 0.3 of it ([arXiv 2610.30783](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.30783)). It is mono, vocal-plus-backing only, and its code is promised but not yet released.

### Why a "listening" model is not the answer yet

General audio-language models cannot yet judge a mix. Several 2026 benchmarks agree that they recognise content but sit **near chance on relative loudness, dynamic range, "which is more X" and severity ordering**, exactly the judgements mixing needs ([SonicBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11039v1); [AcousticBench](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/poster/139542); [MRMAD](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.22236)). On MixJudge's exam, the generative ALMs' severity correlations were 0.175–0.309, against 0.971 for the purpose-trained critic ([arXiv 2610.30783](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.30783)). The information is present in the audio encoders (linear probes recover it), and targeted fine-tuning closes much of the gap: Kimi-Audio went from 69.2% to 98.7% on relative discrimination ([AcousticBench](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/poster/139542)). That is a research project, not a feature. Your own hearing benchmark agrees: measured level changes were detected 8/8 and tonal changes only 4/8 ([docs/audio-intelligence/render-and-measure.md](../docs/audio-intelligence/render-and-measure.md)). Use an ALM, if at all, as a *describer* (genre, instruments, sections), never as a judge. For a shipped app, Qwen3-Omni is Apache-2.0 ([arXiv 2509.17765](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.17765v1)), while Music Flamingo and Audio Flamingo 3 are non-commercial ([HF](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/audio-flamingo-3)).

### What the ears should be: deltas, a loop and a critic

Measurements work best as **deltas against a target, per stem and per section**, not as a quality score. Global features of a stereo mixdown showed "virtually no correlation" with listener preference across 184 mixes ([Colonel & Reiss, AES 2019](https://joshreiss.github.io/documents/2019/20549.pdf)). Meanwhile the defensible acoustic distance in MixJudge is simply **RMS level difference plus octave-band log-energy difference** against a professional reference ([arXiv 2610.30783](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.30783)). Meters are therefore for diagnosis ("vocal 2.1 LU under the reference relative to the beat; 250–500 Hz +3 dB") and for proving that a requested change happened, not for declaring a mix good.

The loop that works is MixJudge's and InstructFX2FX's. Propose a few candidates, render short excerpts, measure or critique each at matched loudness, choose, and stop on a budget. Your vocal run already does exactly this, about 10 s per run offline ([docs/audio-intelligence/vocal-mix-workflow.md](../docs/audio-intelligence/vocal-mix-workflow.md)), and §12 of your harness plan already writes down the right stopping rule: "Do not keep searching until a noisy evaluator eventually says yes" ([docs/audio-intelligence/audio-engineering-harness.md](../docs/audio-intelligence/audio-engineering-harness.md)).

### Evaluating a mix without a listener: four tiers, none sufficient alone

No validated single number for mix quality exists. The usable hierarchy has four tiers:

- **Hard technical gates.** Integrated LUFS on target, true peak ≤ −1 dBTP (−2 if louder than −14 LUFS, per [Spotify](https://support.spotify.com/us/artists/article/loudness-normalization/)), no clipping, LRA not collapsed, mono-compatible low end. These are pass/fail.
- **Reference-relative deltas.** Per-stem loudness and band energy against a reference track, or against a held-out professional mix of the same multitrack when you have one.
- **An issue critic.** MixJudge-style, trained on synthetic degradations of stems you own.
- **Set-level metrics** for regression testing across many songs. KAD over effect embeddings is the 2025–26 standard ([arXiv 2502.15602](https://pdf.arxiv.org/abs/2502.15602)), but it compares sets, not single mixes, and is biased toward any system built on the same embedding ([MEGAMI](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.08040)).

General "aesthetics" predictors are not mix judges. An independent (though commercially interested) review found Audiobox Aesthetics' production-quality score insensitive to mastering fidelity, rewarding "conventional pop characteristics" ([Cyanite](https://cyanite.ai/blog/meta-audiobox-aesthetics-review/)). The final arbiter remains a human. You may be weak at *making* mixes, but you can still reliably *prefer* one in a blind, level-matched A/B, and that preference is the ground truth the system should collect.

## The architecture: a measured rule engine your assistant drives

### Four layers, each matched to what the evidence says it is good at

The codebase is closer to this than most DAWs. The whole mix is JSON in the document: 10 effect slots per insert, sends, sidechains, VCA groups and automation. Pro-Q (24 bands, dynamic, linear phase, EQ Match), Pro-C (21 styles, external sidechain), a reverb with ducking, and YT-L (true peak, BS.1770 meter, already with "Streaming −14" and "Streaming Loud" presets) are all native ([docs/claude/music-effects.md](../docs/claude/music-effects.md); [shared/music/pro-l-presets.ts](../shared/music/pro-l-presets.ts)). BS.1770 integrated loudness, LRA, true peak and 7-band balance are already pure TypeScript ([shared/music/audio-intelligence/loudness.ts](../shared/music/audio-intelligence/loudness.ts); [spectrum.ts](../shared/music/audio-intelligence/spectrum.ts)). So the auto-mixer writes **ordinary, undoable document edits**, which is what Neutron and RoEx do and the only design that keeps the result editable. Mastering needs orchestration, not new DSP.

| Layer | Job | Built from | Why this and not something smarter |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Input and roles | Turn MP3, stems or .flp into "a song whose channels are stems", each tagged with a role and bus | HTDemucs (existing), stem import, `fillMixerInserts` roles, insert names | Labels are what made KEAMS reach pro parity; grouping is what made 2026's two-stage mixers win |
| 2. Rule planner | Gain staging, HPF, group then global balance, gated corrective EQ, light unmasking, Ma-2015 compression, role panning, one shared space | Pure functions in `shared/music/audio-intelligence/` | Rules ≈ pros in small tests; neural parameter estimators are worse on real sessions |
| 3. Verify loop | Render candidates offline, measure deltas, level-match, accept or reject, one undo step | Generalised `MixRun` / vocal run, isolated offline engine | The literature's "ear": InstructFX2FX, MixJudge, your vocal run |
| 4. LLM | Intent, priorities, words → measurable targets, explanation, refinements | MCP tools over layers 1–3 | LLMs handle intent well and abstract-to-parameter mapping badly (RIME) |

**Layer 1: inputs.** Stems are the input that matters. A folder of WAVs (from FL's "Split mixer tracks" or any DAW) becomes one channel and one insert per file. An **MP3** goes through the existing HTDemucs path. Separation gives about 9 dB SDR (RoFormer models about 9.65, [ZFTurbo](https://github.com/ZFTurbo/Music-Source-Separation-Training/blob/main/docs/pretrained_models.md)), which is good enough for *coarse* rebalancing but not heavy per-stem processing. So an MP3 should get a gentle rebalance with a residual stem (mix minus the sum of stems) kept to restore what the separator lost, or default to mastering only. An **.flp** cannot give you audio, because plugin states are opaque and FL-only ([PyFLP](https://github.com/demberto/PyFLP)). Parse it for metadata only (tempo, insert names, routing, fader and pan starting points) to seed the stem mix and pre-label roles. FL's command line renders a full mixdown (`/R /E /F`), which suits mastering, but no switch for split mixer tracks was found ([Image-Line forum](https://forum.image-line.com/viewtopic.php?p=1210668)). For a song made inside YTStudio itself, the safest route is to render each channel once in the offline engine and then iterate only on mixer processing. Analysis renders currently refuse third-party plugins, because their state restoration isn't verified ([server/music/audio-intelligence/service.ts](../server/music/audio-intelligence/service.ts)).

**Layer 2: the planner, in the order the evidence ranks it.**

1. **Gain staging and HPF per role.**
2. **Grouping** into drums, bass, music, vocals and FX buses. Get this right first.
3. **Intra-group equal local loudness**, then **inter-group role offsets** measured with gated BS.1770 (vocal ≈ −3 LU, drums and bass per genre).
4. **EQ candidates from the literature, applied only where measurement shows the excess** (fixing KEAMS's blind-EQ failure). Then a masking pass that cuts ≤ 3–6 dB on the lower-priority track, preferably as a Pro-Q dynamic band keyed to the vocal, using the sidechain you already have.
5. **Light compression** from the Ma 2015 formulas, erring toward too little.
6. **Role panning** with the sine/cosine law, mostly for mono sources: producers' synths usually arrive stereo.
7. **One shared reverb at about −14 LU**, with delay or reverb time set by bounded ST-ITO search, the one place your pilot showed search beats guessing.

The EQ Match fit and collision detection in [src/suites/music/pro-q/match.ts](../src/suites/music/pro-q/match.ts) should move to `shared/` so the server-side planner can use it.

**Layer 3: the verify loop** generalises the vocal run to every stem and to the master bus. It should run in an **isolated offline engine** like `renderSongPreview`'s, which builds its own `AudioEngine` with `offline: true` and a throwaway state copy ([server/music/project-preview.ts](../server/music/project-preview.ts)). Then it doesn't take the audio device or queue behind exports, and it escapes the 120 s passage cap of the shared-engine path ([docs/audio-intelligence/engine-render-limits.md](../docs/audio-intelligence/engine-render-limits.md)). Every candidate is compared **level-matched**, on at least two passages (for example verse and drop), against the untouched mix and against the equal-loudness floor. A candidate that doesn't measurably move toward its target, or that regresses a hard gate, is dropped. "No change" is a legitimate answer.

**Mastering** is a separate stage on the master bus, and the best-supported open design is Matchering's, reimplemented from its published description because the code is GPL-3.0:

1. Match the RMS of the loudest ~15 s sections to the reference.
2. Take the mid/side average spectra, form the ratio, LOWESS-smooth it on a log-frequency grid, and turn it into a **linear-phase FIR**.
3. Make a few gain-correction passes through the limiter, so its clipping is accounted for ([matchering stages.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sergree/matchering/master/matchering/stages.py)).

In YTStudio terms that means EQ Match or a linear-phase Pro-Q toward the reference, gentle glue compression (2:1–4:1, 1–3 dB, slow attack, about 500 ms release, per the KEAMS mix-bus rules), then YT-L iterated to target: −14 LUFS / −1 dBTP by default, with "loud" (−9 to −11) as an explicit choice. Spotify raises quiet tracks only as far as their headroom allows and turns everything down to −14, so extra loudness buys nothing there ([Spotify](https://support.spotify.com/us/artists/article/loudness-normalization/)). Without a reference, fall back to the nearest of a few target curves by spectral distance, phaselimiter's trick ([ai-mastering/phaselimiter](https://github.com/ai-mastering/phaselimiter)). Build those curves from the user's own chosen favourites rather than a vendor's genre list, since no open, licensed per-genre curve set exists.

**Layer 4: the assistant** gets the tools the literature says it lacks:

- **`analyze_mix`**: per stem, per bus and per section, a compact table of LUFS, true peak, LRA, crest, band energy relative to the mix and to the reference, low-end mono, masking pairs and the vocal-to-beat ratio, all as deltas.
- **`compare_to_reference`.**
- **`try_candidates`**: render N excerpts and return the measured deltas.
- **`optimise_params`**: a bounded search, held to presets or the LLM's own starting point so it can't drift.
- **`auto_mix`** and **`auto_master`**, each with a matching UI action, to satisfy the parity rule.

The prompt should say that every change is measured after it is made and that no result is claimed unmeasured. Fix the prompt's contradiction about whether LUFS is available, too ([server/suites/music.ts](../server/suites/music.ts)).

## A phased plan: rules and meters first, models last

**Phase 0: give the assistant eyes on the numbers (days).** Add levels, LUFS and band balance to `describeMixer` / `get_mixer`, or add `analyze_mix`. Fix the prompt contradiction. Require measure-after-change. This alone attacks the "generic and guessing" complaint at its root, because the assistant can only be as specific as what it can see.

**Phase 1: mastering, end to end.** It is the smallest scope with the strongest evidence and almost all the DSP already exists. Build `auto_master` on stereo input (an MP3, an FL mixdown, or your own song's master bus): reference-match by the reimplemented Matchering algorithm, glue compression, YT-L iterated to a chosen target, then hard gates and a gain-matched A/B in the UI. Ship it as one undoable chain of native processors.

**Phase 2: the balance mixer (the priority).** Build stem import, role tagging confirmed by the user, grouping into buses, intra- then inter-group loudness balance, HPF, light Ma-2015 compression, role panning and one reverb send at about −14 LU, all verified by the generalised `MixRun` loop in an isolated offline engine and applied as one undo step. Equal local loudness is the floor it must beat. Balance and space are where 64% of mix criticism lands, so this phase should deliver most of the perceived improvement.

**Phase 3: corrective EQ and unmasking.** Add measurement-gated literature EQ, priority-based masking cuts with dynamic Pro-Q bands keyed by sidechain, and EQ Match moved to `shared/`. Then the .flp metadata reader, written in TypeScript or starting from the MIT ts-flp ([ts-flp](https://github.com/SpendLessDaw/ts-flp)), and the MP3 path with a residual stem.

**Phase 4: search where rules are weakest.** Add bounded ST-ITO-style search for delay and reverb, and reference-style matching per bus, held by preset priors.

**Phase 5: learned parts, only for demonstrated gaps.** This matches your backlog's "train only for demonstrated gaps" slice ([docs/audio-intelligence/implementation-backlog.md](../docs/audio-intelligence/implementation-backlog.md)). Train a MixJudge-style critic on synthetic degradations of stems you own, rendered through your own engine. If and when rules plateau, train a parameter predictor on synthetic (dry, wet, parameters) renders. Ship both as ONNX in the existing `ml-worker.mjs`, never Python.

**Skip** end-to-end audio models as the engine, Diff-MST, general ALMs as judges, Audiobox scores as quality, and bundling PyFLP. Treat time-varying automation as later work: every published automixer, MEGAMI included, makes static decisions.

**Testing quality without ears** comes in four layers:

1. **Unit tests** of each pure planner on synthetic fixtures: a tone at a known level must come out at the right offset, and a pink-noise mask must yield a cut in the right band.
2. **Hard gates** on every render.
3. **A benchmark** of 20–30 multitracks whose professional mix you have, from your own past songs or properly licensed multitracks (Cambridge-MT's terms could not be verified). Score each auto-mix by per-stem loudness and octave-band distance to the pro mix, against equal loudness, the current assistant and no processing, and track KAD across versions to catch regressions.
4. **A built-in blind A/B** at matched loudness, logging your choices. That gives the final verdict and the personalisation data in one.

Never trust an improvement that only shows up unmatched for level.

## Conclusion

The evidence reframes the problem. The obstacle to a good auto-mixer in YTStudio is not a missing breakthrough model. It is that the assistant has been asked to mix from a description of the routing, while the systems that work all mix from measurements against a target and then check their work. The most valuable insight from 2026 research is organisational, not neural: get the roles and groups right, balance inside groups before across them, err dry and lightly compressed, and let a render-and-compare loop veto any change it cannot show helps. A DAW that owns its processors, its offline renderer and its document can do all of this deterministically, explain every move and undo it, which no cloud service or audio-to-audio model can offer.

What remains unproven matters for setting expectations. No published study has had listeners judge an LLM-driven mix of a full multitrack song. The rule targets come from rock and pop, not beat-driven genres. The best listening tests involved 12 to 26 people on a handful of songs. The honest goal is therefore a mixer that is reliably clean, balanced and loud enough, and that improves as it learns your blind preferences, not one that beats an engineer. Build the floor first, and measure every step above it.
